== The idea

Ashby's law: to realize specified effects "there must exist as much
variety in the strategies available as there is variety in the
conditions that obtain" (Lewis & Aligica 2024, p. 6). A regulator can
only absorb the disturbances it has moves to match. Vincent Ostrom
took this directly from conversations with Ashby at Stanford
(1955–56): governance systems must be at least as complex as the
systems they govern; "Ashby's theory of adaptive behaviour is today a
basic foundation of my own work" (p. 6).

Elinor's 1995 statement: any governance system regulating complex
biological systems "must have as much variety in the actions that it
can take as there exists in the systems being regulated" (p. 6). Lewis
& Aligica read the central empirical finding of Governing the Commons
— that specific rules-in-use differ markedly case to case — as itself
an instance of the law (pp. 6–7): rule diversity is functional
variety, not noise, and it commends institutional arrangements at
diverse scales rather than one best design. Digging Deeper supplies
the raw magnitude: 27+ boundary rules in use, a rule set too large for
any analyst to analyze completely (Ostrom 2005/2025, pp. 90–91).

== The Nostr case

Nostr's exclusion machinery — mutes, reports, client filters, WoT
scoring, paid entry, PoW, allowlists, report-driven policy — is an
open, self-renewing variety pool matched against an adversarial,
self-renewing disturbance pool (spam, abuse, sybils). The project's
"unenumerability is the finding" discipline is requisite variety
taken seriously: a complete catalogue of exclusion methods would be
evidence the system had *stopped* matching its environment. Strain:
Ashby's regulator is one system with many moves; Nostr's variety is
distributed across uncoordinated actors — variety of *regulators*,
not just of moves (link:claim-multiplicity-stack.adoc[claim-multiplicity-stack], link:q-decoupled-vs-unitary-sanctions.adoc[q-decoupled-vs-unitary-sanctions]).
