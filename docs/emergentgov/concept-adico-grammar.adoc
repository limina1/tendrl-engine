== The idea

Crawford and Ostrom's grammar parses institutional statements into
Attributes (whom it applies to), Deontic (may/must/must not), aIm (the
action/outcome), Conditions, and Or Else — the actor "given the
responsibility of imposing sanctions" (McGinnis pp. 13–14). Strategies
` AIC; norms ` ADIC; rules = full ADICO (p. 14). Norms carry no Or
Else: they are enforced through socialization and internalized delta
parameters, effective only "if a norm is widely shared" (p. 14).
Following Commons, every right in a rule implies a correlative duty on
a designated enforcer (p. 14) — the O slot is where enforcement is
institutionally located. Cf. Ostrom 2007, p. 23 (rules "predictably
enforced... by agents responsible for monitoring conduct and for
imposing sanctions").

== The Nostr engagement

A kind-1984 report is an ADIC statement: it names an attribute-bearer
(the reported pubkey), a deontic charge, an aim, conditions — and no Or
Else. By the grammar it is a norm, not a rule. Yet it gets enforced,
because uncoordinated third parties (relay operators, client filters,
label subscribers) each supply their own O according to their own
payoff rules: the Or Else component is crowdsourced rather than
assigned. This is the grammatical statement of decoupled sanctions —
the reporter's act is a costly, public, non-binding signal; enforcement
is a separate actor's separate decision (link:claim-split-sanctions.adoc[claim-split-sanctions],
link:q-decoupled-vs-unitary-sanctions.adoc[q-decoupled-vs-unitary-sanctions]). It also strains the grammar's own
sociology: McGinnis says O-less statements work only where a community
shares the norm; Nostr enforcement instead rides heterogeneous private
incentives of operators — a mechanism ADICO doesn't anticipate.
