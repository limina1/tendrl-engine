== What it is

Intellectual history arguing that the Ostroms' theory of
self-governance was built on cybernetics — control via feedback —
absorbed chiefly from Ross Ashby (Vincent knew him at Stanford,
1955–56). Supplies the mechanism-language behind polycentricity,
requisite variety, and the three levels of rules.

== Core claims

1. pp. 1–2 — Ashby gave the Ostroms three things: requisite variety;
   the necessity of a *hierarchy* of feedback mechanisms for
   self-organization; and the rationale for Buchanan & Tullock's
   levels-of-rules distinction.
2. p. 3 — polycentric systems are "more likely than monocentric
   systems to provide incentives leading to self-organized,
   self-corrective institutional change" (Ostrom 1998).
3. p. 4 — self-governed systems are complex adaptive systems adapting
   "by changing the rules dynamically on the basis of experience";
   competition among overlapping providers yields error-correcting
   negative feedback as an unintended consequence.
4. p. 6 — Vincent: "Ashby's theory of adaptive behaviour is today a
   basic foundation of my own work."
5. pp. 6–7 — Elinor 1995: a governance system regulating complex
   systems "must have as much variety in the actions that it can
   take" as the regulated system; the cross-case diversity of
   rules-in-use in Governing the Commons instantiates the law.
6. pp. 7–8 — homeostatic model: circular causality + tendency toward
   stability + information flows linking them; negative feedback is
   error-correcting, positive feedback is runaway.
7. pp. 9–10 — the Ashby challenge: no first-order machine can switch
   itself from error-aggravating to error-mitigating feedback; a
   meta-level variable "alpha" is required — "the part can be
   self-organizing within the whole S + α" (p. 9); hence compounded
   hierarchies of feedback, Hofstadter strange loops (p. 10).
8. p. 10 — the three rule levels are "an expression of the underlying
   logic of the very phenomenon of self-organization" per cybernetics,
   not mere induction from federalism.
9. p. 11 — Vincent restates Madison's checks as "the general veto
   theorem formulated by W. Ross Ashby in Design for a Brain";
   fn. 14: overlapping units transmit what worked; larger systems back
   up failing small ones.
10. pp. 12–13 — level-shifting (using collective-choice rules to
    change operational rules) *is* the negative-feedback / alpha
    mechanism in IAD terms; fn. 19: those who cannot level-shift are
    "stuck in a single-tier world."

== Tensions / open questions

- The paper's alpha is vertical; Nostr's uncoordinated multi-layer
  exclusion is lateral and plural — link:concept-adaptive-governance.adoc[concept-adaptive-governance],
  link:claim-multiplicity-stack.adoc[claim-multiplicity-stack].
- Homeostasis presumes a shared setpoint; Nostr has none —
  substrate- vs community-homeostasis, see link:q-generativity-robustness.adoc[q-generativity-robustness].
- Veto theorem inverted: on Nostr no part has veto over the whole —
  link:q-decoupled-vs-unitary-sanctions.adoc[q-decoupled-vs-unitary-sanctions].
- Mechanism-language without observational method — Nostr is the
  observatory the cybernetic account never had — link:concept-survivorship-bias.adoc[concept-survivorship-bias].
