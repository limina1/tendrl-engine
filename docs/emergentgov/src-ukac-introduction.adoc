== What it is

The book's definitional apparatus: knowledge as "a resource shared by
a group of people that is subject to social dilemmas" (3), the goods
typology, the Hardin corrective, the two intellectual histories of
the field, and — crucially for this project — the survivorship hedge
on the design principles.

== Core claims

1. (7) THE SURVIVORSHIP HEDGE, prominent: the eight design principles
   (Ostrom 1990) "were discovered after conducting a large set of
   empirical studies" of already-robust vs. failed systems; "they are
   in no way prescriptive—nor are they models"; findings from "small,
   homogeneous systems. Whether they apply to the study of large and
   complex systems like the knowledge commons is a question for
   further research." See link:concept-survivorship-bias.adoc[concept-survivorship-bias],
   link:concept-design-principles.adoc[concept-design-principles].
2. (4) The information-commons movement began ~1995 as "a spontaneous
   explosion of 'ah ha' moments" — dilemmas (congestion, free riding,
   conflict, pollution) noticed before theory.
3. (4–5) Commons (general term) vs. common-pool resource (a good) vs.
   common property (a legal regime) — link:concept-cpr-vs-knowledge-commons.adoc[concept-cpr-vs-knowledge-commons].
4. (5) Subtractability inversion: "the more people who share useful
   knowledge, the greater the common good"; threats are
   "commodification or enclosure, pollution and degradation, and
   nonsustainability."
5. (8–9) Two-dimensional goods schema (V. & E. Ostrom 1977):
   exclusion × subtractability; Figure 1.1 (9) puts useful knowledge
   with public goods, libraries with CPRs.
6. (10) "New technologies can enable the capture of what were once
   free and open public goods," converting them into CPRs "that
   need[] to be managed, monitored, and protected."
7. (10–11) HARDIN'S FOUR ERRORS: (1) described open access, not a
   managed commons; (2) assumed no communication; (3) assumed pure
   immediate self-interest; (4) offered only privatization or
   government. "Au contraire, Monsieur Hardin!"
8. (11) Heller's anticommons: underuse of scarce scientific resources
   via excessive IP and overpatenting.
9. (12–13) Two intellectual histories: enclosure (Boyle's "intangible
   commons of the mind," code-as-law, DMCA/TRIPS/CTEA) vs.
   openness/democracy (town commons, Benkler, libraries as
   "strongholds of democracy").
10. (14) "[A] commons is not value laden—its outcome can be good or
    bad, sustainable or not"; commons ≠ open access; OA of information
    ≠ Hardin's free-for-all (13).
11. (14) DIGITAL FRAGILITY: knowledge in digital form is "more
    vulnerable than ever before"; with hard copy "the decentralization
    of multiple copies made the works robust"; licensed digital works
    are "centralized and vulnerable to the whims or happenstance of
    the publisher."
12. (19) LIBERTARIAN VS. ASSOCIATIONAL COMMONS (Levine): anyone-can-
    access vs. member-open; commons "need protection by groups
    interested in their production, care, and maintenance."
13. (20–21) Agenda questions: "How do we evaluate how we are doing?
    How do we monitor our progress? Who should govern the Internet?"
