== What it is

Frames digital preservation (of e-journals) as a common-pool-resource
problem: scholarly communities are "fabrics of trust" held together by
checkable references, and nobody has private incentive to keep the
referents alive. Surveys who could archive (producer, consumer,
third-party) and lands on community-based, club-good solutions
(Portico, LOCKSS).

== Core claims

1. "The links work, the trust endures, and the commons nourishes the
   intellectual life only when the reader is able to check the
   reference at the other end" (pp. 146-147).
2. "Access is not equivalent to preservation" (p. 149): open access
   encourages use today but motivates no one to preserve for tomorrow.
3. Preservation defeats the insurance/exclusion logic: if I archive
   and you don't, you still benefit — free riding is near-total, so
   markets underprovide it (p. 150).
4. Hardin's pessimism is empirically unwarranted: "groups of people
   with a common interest in a shared resource will devise and agree
   on community-based mechanisms" for preservation (p. 151).
5. Producer archives are structurally untrustworthy: Elsevier's
   "vanishing act" removed 35+ articles; online custody invites quiet
   retraction — "Are there pieces of articles that I would quietly
   change if I could? Well, interesting thought, sure" (O'Donnell,
   qtd. p. 155).
6. LOCKSS ("Lots Of Copies Keeps Stuff Safe"): distributed caches
   "checking each other to see whether files are damaged or lost and
   repairing any damage," removing single points of failure (p. 157).
7. Community archives face a two-sided-market bootstrap (publishers
   won't join without libraries, and vice versa) and solve free riding
   by organizing as clubs whose good is guaranteed future access
   (pp. 158-159).

== Key concepts

Fabrics of trust; access vs preservation; producer/consumer/archive
role separation (five organizational models); vanishing-act problem;
redundant peer-auditing caches; preservation as club good; two-sided
market; dark archives and trigger events.

== Hooks to the Nostr thesis

- Claim 6 is the LOCKSS principle Nostr implements natively: an event
  broadcast to N relays is its own distributed archive — direct
  ancestry for link:claim-multiplicity-stack.adoc[claim-multiplicity-stack] and the redundancy half of
  link:claim-no-global-bannability.adoc[claim-no-global-bannability] (no single point of deletion, not
  just of exclusion).
- Claim 5 (custodian can quietly rewrite the record) is exactly what
  signed events foreclose: kind-5 deletion is advisory, edits are
  detectable, provenance is cryptographic. The "vanishing act" becomes
  observable rather than silent — feeds
  link:claim-formation-observable.adoc[claim-formation-observable].
- Role separation (claim 7's producer/consumer/archive) maps onto the
  decoupling motif of link:claim-split-sanctions.adoc[claim-split-sanctions]: on Nostr the author,
  the relay (custodian), and the reporting/muting public are
  independent actors whose interests never fully align.
- Claims 2-3 name a gap in relay economics: relays serve _access_;
  nothing in the protocol pays for _preservation_. Club-good archival
  relays (paid, member-gated retention guarantees) would be a
  predictable institutional response — a bounded institution per
  link:claim-composable-bounded-institutions.adoc[claim-composable-bounded-institutions].

== Tensions / open questions

- LOCKSS caches audit and _repair_ each other under one protocol
  charter; Nostr relays are uncoordinated and free to drop anything —
  redundancy without mutual obligation. Is emergent replication
  durable, or does it decay like link rot? →
  link:q-preservation-without-archives.adoc[q-preservation-without-archives]
- Waters' club solution reintroduces exclusion to fund a public good;
  on Nostr this collides with the ethos of open relays — a boundary
  question for link:q-boundary-sybil.adoc[q-boundary-sybil] adjacent economics.
- Retraction has legitimate uses (error, plagiarism, dangerous data);
  an unerasable commons must govern _correction_ without erasure.
