== The idea

Ostrom's central methodological obstacle: "Institutions themselves are
usually invisible" — they are shared concepts existing "in the minds
of participants," often implicit rather than written (Ostrom
2005/2025, p. 78). The rules that actually govern — rules-in-use —
are the do's and don'ts learned on the ground, absent from any
document and sometimes contrary to the written rules (pp. 79, 86).
The analyst reaches them only by fieldwork: asking how X is done here
and why Y is not done, watching who is called on to justify what.
Hence institutional analysis has been, of necessity, retrospective
ethnography — and of survivors (link:concept-survivorship-bias.adoc[concept-survivorship-bias]).

== The inversion

On Nostr the rules-in-use have a wire format. A mute is a signed
kind-10000 event; a report is a kind-1984 event with a category tag; a
follow, a relay policy document, an allowlist change — each is a
typed, signed, timestamped artifact on the open substrate. The
operative prescriptions are not (only) in participants' heads: the
acts that express and enforce them are published as data. This is the
methodological anchor of link:claim-formation-observable.adoc[claim-formation-observable]: governance
formation can be watched, not reconstructed.

The inversion is partial, and that is worth keeping precise. What the
wire carries is behavior under rules, plus some rules-in-form (relay
policies, NIPs); the shared *meanings* — what counts as "spam" in a
1984 report, which mutes are norm-enforcement vs taste — remain
interpretive, exactly Ostrom's shared-meaning problem (p. 87). And
the record adds a wrinkle her framework never faced: a rule is
rescindable "or it is not a rule" (p. 86), but the signed history of
rule changes is append-only and un-rescindable.
