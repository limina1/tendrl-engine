== What it is

Applies the IAD framework to FOSS projects as common-property regimes
and asks whether the FOSS paradigm (Copyleft licensing + Internet
collaboration infrastructure) can be transplanted to science generally
("open content"). The one UKAC chapter that analyzes governance of
digitally-native, self-organizing commons — closest ancestor to
analyzing Nostr.

== Core claims

1. FOSS inverts the classic CPR problem: collective action to _create_
   a public good, not to prevent overappropriation (p. 279).
2. The licensing innovation is institutional, not legal abolition: "It
   is not a question of choosing between the two extremes of full
   copyright ... or no copyright at all" (p. 283) — authors unbundle
   and selectively relinquish rights.
3. Rules-in-use are partly executed by infrastructure: "the CVS system
   actually articulates some of the project's operational rules and
   enforces them" (p. 287).
4. Unlike journals, FOSS shares the whole process — full history,
   rollback, attribution tracking — not just final results (p. 288).
5. Governance forms vary widely: benevolent dictator + lieutenants
   (Linux), elected nine-member team (FreeBSD), consensus; "an ongoing
   experiment ... imperfect mix of leadership, informal coordination
   mechanisms, implicit and explicit norms" (Weber, qtd. pp. 283-284).
6. Projects follow a trajectory: initiation → "going-open" → high
   growth _ stabilization _ death; high growth is rare, most projects
   are tiny (pp. 280-281).
7. Contributor motives: technological need, sociopolitical movement,
   economic signaling/human capital; attribution norms are what make
   signaling work (pp. 284-285, 295).

== Key concepts

Copyleft as rules-in-use; viral licensing; version control as rule
enforcement; three-stage commons trajectory; modularity as physical
attribute enabling parallel contribution; benevolent-dictator vs
elected-committee governance; attribution as incentive substrate.

== Hooks to the Nostr thesis

- Claim 3 is the sharpest precedent for
  link:concept-governance-as-software.adoc[concept-governance-as-software]: relay policy code, NIP-42 auth,
  and mute-list plugins are CVS-write-authority writ large.
- The initiation→going-open→mature trajectory is a testable template
  for link:q-maturation-hypothesis.adoc[q-maturation-hypothesis] and gives
  link:claim-formation-observable.adoc[claim-formation-observable] a comparative baseline — except FOSS
  history had to be reconstructed; Nostr's is a signed event stream.
- License variation (50+ OSI licenses from one GPL parent) parallels
  link:claim-relay-spectrum.adoc[claim-relay-spectrum]: a family of institutional variants on one
  substrate, chosen per-project/per-relay.
- Attribution-as-incentive (claims 4, 7) maps to Nostr's signed,
  pubkey-attributed events: the signaling economy is native.
- Forking under viral licenses = exit with the resource; cf.
  link:concept-exit-voice.adoc[concept-exit-voice] and exit-based operator discipline.

== Tensions / open questions

- Schweik treats governance design as deliberate and per-project;
  Nostr's exclusion is redundant and _uncoordinated_ across layers —
  who is the "lead developer" of the open pool? (cf.
  link:q-decoupled-vs-unitary-sanctions.adoc[q-decoupled-vs-unitary-sanctions])
- FOSS boundaries are commit-access boundaries (writable core, open
  periphery) — structurally the write-gated/readable-everywhere
  pattern of link:claim-composable-bounded-institutions.adoc[claim-composable-bounded-institutions], but Schweik never
  theorizes it as such.
- His financing worry (volunteer fragility, foundation/firm capture)
  transfers directly to relay economics.
