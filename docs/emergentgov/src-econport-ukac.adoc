== What it is

Case study of EconPort, an open-access digital library + online
experimental-economics laboratory, analyzed as "a case study of the
effectiveness of incentives for creation, maintenance, and
utilization" of a knowledge commons (p. 334). The book's most concrete
micro-institutional account of one bounded knowledge facility.

== Core claims

1. Pre-Internet, the LAN-bound lab was self-limiting but stable: "The
   very local nature of the public-good elements of the resource made
   it relatively easy to solve the free-rider problem" (p. 337) —
   boundedness itself did the institutional work.
2. Demand side: EconPort is a "local public good" globally consumable
   — usable only by coordinated communities who can convene
   simultaneous participants (p. 341).
3. Supply side: it is an associational, not libertarian, commons:
   "Open-source software is a supply-side example of a libertarian
   commons ... In contrast, the supply side of a digital library such
   as EconPort is ... associational" (p. 341, citing Levine).
4. Sustainability tracks whether stewards use the resource in their
   own work: economists (users) have unconditional continuing
   interest; the AI Lab (funded builders) has none post-grant
   (pp. 342-344).
5. Outsiders don't contribute despite open APIs, plausibly "fearing
   that their work would become disassociated from their identities"
   or that the site will die (p. 344) — attribution and
   institutional-longevity fears gate contribution.
6. Fallback: collect _pointers_ with classification metadata rather
   than hosting content (pp. 344-345) — index, don't ingest.
7. When the university defunded it, the creators relocated and took
   the commons with them to Georgia State; "universities are not
   always willing to allocate sufficient funds" even for highly rated
   commons (p. 346).

== Key concepts

Local public good; insider/outsider incentive asymmetry;
steward-as-user sustainability condition; pointer-collection vs
hosting; grant-cycle fragility.

== Hooks to the Nostr thesis

- The chapter is nearly an allegory of the medical-relay motif in
  link:claim-composable-bounded-institutions.adoc[claim-composable-bounded-institutions]: a write-gated facility
  (insider-produced experiment software) whose outputs are globally
  readable, run by the community that needs it.
- Claim 1 → link:concept-boundary-rules.adoc[concept-boundary-rules]: shrinking the boundary dissolves
  the free-rider problem; widening it re-creates commons dilemmas.
  Relay allowlists are the same dial.
- Claim 5 is evidence for attribution as a contribution precondition
  — Nostr events are signed and portable, so identity can never be
  disassociated from content and no single site's death orphans it:
  the protocol dissolves both stated fears. Feeds
  link:claim-formation-observable.adoc[claim-formation-observable] (incentive design is legible in the
  event stream).
- Claim 6 anticipates the relay/index architecture: point, classify,
  relay — don't centrally host.
- Claim 7 is exit-based discipline inverted: the _stewards_ exited the
  host institution and the commons survived by portability — cf.
  link:concept-exit-voice.adoc[concept-exit-voice],
  link:concept-interjurisdictional-competition.adoc[concept-interjurisdictional-competition],
  link:q-does-competition-improve-governance.adoc[q-does-competition-improve-governance].

== Tensions / open questions

- Sustainability here depends on a durable employer-subsidized
  steward; Nostr relays lack tenure — is steward-as-user (claim 4)
  enough to keep hobbyist relays alive, or does the multiplicity stack
  (link:claim-multiplicity-stack.adoc[claim-multiplicity-stack]) make any single relay's death cheap?
- EconPort's community never materialized as contributors (one-way
  commons). What distinguishes commons that recruit co-producers from
  those that stay staff-fed?
