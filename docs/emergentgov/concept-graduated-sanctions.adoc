== The idea

Design principle 5: "graduated sanctions are applied to rule violators
(in increasing levels of intensity)" (McGinnis p. 19), paired with
principle 4 — monitors accountable to, or identical with, the
appropriators. In IAD's machinery sanctions live in the payoff rules,
whose diagnostic questions presume a located enforcer: "How is
conformance to rules monitored? Who is responsible for sanctioning
nonconformers? How reliably are sanctions imposed?" (Ostrom 2007,
p. 39). The classic findings assume this monitor–sanctioner nexus is
internal and unitary: user-monitors outperform hired guards (p. 50);
externally imposed sanction systems "crowd the benefits of a
self-imposed set of sanctions"; boundedly rational users deploy
"measured responses" — graduation — to stabilize agreements with no
external enforcer (p. 48).

== The Nostr engagement

Nostr splits the nexus. Monitoring is a public, competitive activity
(reports, labels, WoT scores — cf. McGinnis p. 18, many units monitor
other units); sanctioning is a separate, private decision by whoever
runs a relay or client. A kind-1984 report is costly and public but
binds no one — a norm-shaped statement without an Or Else
(link:concept-adico-grammar.adoc[concept-adico-grammar]). Graduation is therefore not designed but
emergent-in-aggregate: a violator loses one relay, then several, then
most clients' defaults — intensity rises with the number of independent
enforcers persuaded, not with a sanction ladder any single institution
maintains. Whether this emergent graduation reproduces the
error-tolerance benefits Ostrom attributes to designed graduation is
the open question: link:q-decoupled-vs-unitary-sanctions.adoc[q-decoupled-vs-unitary-sanctions],
link:claim-split-sanctions.adoc[claim-split-sanctions]. The crowding-out finding (p. 48) doubles as
an argument against re-centralizing moderation on top of Nostr.
