== The idea

Institutions are shared concepts living "in the minds of the
participants" — invisible (Ostrom 2007, p. 23). So institutional
analysis targets _rules-in-use_, not _rules-in-form_: "the dos and
don'ts that one learns on the ground that may not exist in any written
document," which may even contradict the written rules (p. 23).
Working rules are "the set of rules to which participants would make
reference if asked to explain and justify their actions" (p. 36).
McGinnis's shorthand: formal rules ("rules-on-paper") vs the repertoire
of strategies, norms, and rules actually used (p. 12).

The methodological cost is high: rules-in-use aren't in documents,
aren't survey-able, often aren't conceptualized as rules by
participants; eliciting them means fieldwork — asking "how X is done
here and why Y is not done here" (p. 23) via "nonthreatening,
context-specific questions" over time at a site (p. 39; IFRI protocols,
n. 5 pp. 53–54).

== The Nostr engagement

This is the load-bearing methodological hook for the whole project. On
Nostr a large share of rules-in-use are enacted as signed, timestamped,
typed events — mutes, reports, follows, relay policy docs, allowlist
changes — so the rules-in-use/rules-in-form gap becomes directly
measurable (compare a relay's posted policy with its observed
accept/reject behavior). Ostrom's ethnographic elicitation problem is
replaced by a log-reading problem: link:claim-formation-observable.adoc[claim-formation-observable],
link:claim-interviews-carry-claim.adoc[claim-interviews-carry-claim], link:concept-institutional-invisibility.adoc[concept-institutional-invisibility].
Residual invisibility remains (operator intentions, out-of-band norms
in group chats) — the gap the interviews still have to carry.
