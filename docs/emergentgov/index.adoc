== The narrative

This is the working narrative of the program — the essay most will
read and the thing to refine. Everything after it is the
organizational map it draws on; every load-bearing move links to the
zettel that backs it.

=== The question

Nostr is, plausibly, the most adversarial social landscape that
exists. A user is nothing but a public key that signs events. There
is no global authority; any relay can admit anyone; an actor filtered
off one relay reappears on the next. In institutional terms the open
network runs close to what the IAD tradition calls *default
conditions* — the institution-free baseline in which anyone may act
and anyone may keep whatever they can obtain and defend
(link:concept-action-arena.adoc[action arena]). The first design
principle, the clearly defined boundary that stood behind every
commons in _Governing the Commons_, structurally
link:claim-no-global-bannability.adoc[cannot exist here]: bad
actors are filtered *locally*, never banned *globally*
(link:concept-design-principles.adoc[design principles]).

The usual reading stops there: no boundary, no membership, no
enforcement — an unmanaged free-for-all. But that reading repeats the
first of Hardin's errors as Hess and Ostrom catalogued them —
conflating open access with a managed commons
(link:src-ukac-introduction.adoc[UKAC introduction]). The
empirical fact is that the commons holds. It coheres, it sustains
discourse, and the people who have seen the worst of it stay. One
question therefore organizes everything that follows: *how is there
quality discourse at all?*

=== The founders' limit, and the inversion

The founders of knowledge-commons research named their own limit. In
2007, surveying the new literature, Hess and Ostrom observed that
writers point at outcomes they like or dislike but rarely analyze in
depth: "Thus far we (all of us!) have mainly been at the 'look what
is happening!' stage." Their conclusion made the dependency explicit:
only "after more efforts succeed and others fail" would the field
understand what makes knowledge commons work
(link:src-analyzing-knowledge-commons.adoc[the hinge quotes],
verbatim with locators). Read together, the two passages are
survivorship logic stated in advance. The eight design principles
were read off commons that had *already endured*; they are, in the
editors' own words, "in no way prescriptive," findings from small,
homogeneous systems whose application to large complex commons was
left as an open question
(link:concept-survivorship-bias.adoc[survivorship bias]).
Formation — the stage where rules are being made rather than already
working — was never observable.

Beneath that limit sits the deepest methodological obstacle Ostrom
ever named: institutions are *invisible*. They are shared concepts in
participants' heads; the rules-in-use that actually govern conduct
may exist in no written document and may even contradict the
rules-in-form on paper, so the analyst must dig them out with
fieldwork, site by site
(link:concept-institutional-invisibility.adoc[institutional invisibility],
link:concept-rules-in-use.adoc[rules-in-use]).

Nostr inverts both problems at once. Governance action arrives as
typed, signed, timestamped events: a report is a kind-1984 event, a
mute list is an event, a follow graph is an event, a relay's policy
is a published document, a protocol change is a versioned NIP. The
distinction between rules-in-form and rules-in-use — which the field
could only elicit retrospectively, through interviews about "how X is
done here" — becomes *measurable*: a relay's stated policy against
its observed accept/reject behavior. Even the moment Hess and Ostrom
describe where a working group adopts a new rule "without ever
self-consciously recognizing" it — the quiet drift from operational
action into collective choice
(link:concept-rule-levels.adoc[rule levels]) — is here, more often
than anywhere else, a published artifact. For the first time a
commons can be
link:claim-formation-observable.adoc[watched governing itself into existence].
The working title converts the founders' exclamation into the method:
looking at what is happening becomes analysis when what is happening
arrives as institutional statements on the wire.

=== The object: a framework for formation

Ostrom was careful to distinguish frameworks, theories, and models: a
framework supplies the universal elements and the metatheoretical
language; theories and models make assumptions within it
(link:concept-iad-framework.adoc[IAD framework]). What this work
proposes is a framework — but its object is *emergent governance
itself*, not another resource domain
(link:syn-emergent-governance-framework.adoc[the synthesis note]).
The distinction from the knowledge commons should be drawn precisely,
because it is the paper's claim to a contribution rather than an
application. IAD analyzes an action arena structured by exogenous
variables — rules-in-use, attributes of the community, the
biophysical and technical world — and holds those variables fixed at
the time of analysis; the fixing is a footnote, an acknowledged
convenience. The GKC school loosened it for constructed commons,
adding the feedback loop by which a community's outputs become its
own inputs (link:concept-gkc-framework.adoc[GKC framework]). This
framework takes the remaining step: it makes the feedback loop the
object. Formation is what happens when action situations generate
their own future rules — when participants drift or deliberately
shift from operational play into collective choice and occasionally
into constitutional revision
(link:concept-rule-levels.adoc[rule levels]). Ostrom's theory
treats that level-shift as the elementary act of self-governance;
here it becomes the elementary *observable*. On Nostr the exogeneity
footnote is untenable in the most productive way: rules reshape the
facility set within weeks, so the "held constant" clause fails, and
what fails it is exactly the phenomenon under study.

The unit of analysis is therefore the *formation episode*, sitting
earlier in time than the institution — the analog of the action
arena, one level before rules settle. An episode has an anatomy. It
opens with a *shock*: an exogenous disturbance the current rule
configuration cannot absorb — a spam wave, a coordinated malicious
influx. A *response portfolio* follows: uncoordinated actors across
the stack deploy existing exclusion methods and mint new ones,
a-priori guesses and a-posteriori attestations alike
(link:claim-multiplicity-stack.adoc[multiplicity]). *Adaptation*
registers at every level of rules at once — operational (mutes,
filters, policy toggles), collective-choice (relay policy revisions,
client defaults), sometimes constitutional (a NIP). And the episode
closes in *settlement* — the cycle converges, the boundary
re-expands — or in *failure*: a relay dies, a community disperses, a
method is abandoned. Both endings are data. The failures are
precisely what the survivorship-filtered record never contained, and
they must be archived deliberately, or the bias the framework escapes
at the field level quietly recurs at the archive level
(link:concept-survivorship-bias.adoc[survivorship bias]). The
link:claim-expand-contract.adoc[expand–contract] cycle is the
prototype episode; in IAD terms, an episode is a linked chain of
action situations whose principal outputs are rules.

The architectural section — the breakdown of emergent governance
itself, as distinct from any one substrate — asks what must be true
of a system for formation episodes to be observable at all. Four
conditions. First, actions must be *typed, public, signed, and
timestamped*: institutional statements with a wire format, codable
into the grammar of institutions rather than reconstructed from
recollection. Second, identity must be *portable and separable from
venue*, so that exit is legible rather than silent, and boundary
events are attributable — the observer can tell who left, who was
filtered, and who merely moved. Third, policy and enforcement must be
*separately visible*, so that rules-in-form and rules-in-use can be
compared directly — a stated relay policy against its observed
accept/reject behavior — instead of elicited after the fact
(link:concept-rules-in-use.adoc[rules-in-use]). Fourth, the record
must *persist*: on Nostr effectively irrevocably, so the history of
rule changes — including the reversed and abandoned ones — is part of
the archive rather than of survivors' memories
(link:q-advisory-deletion.adoc[advisory deletion] is the cost of
this same property). Graded against these conditions, git
repositories, wikis, and DAOs are partial observatories; Nostr is,
so far, the maximal one. That grading is what makes the framework
portable rather than a platform study — it tells other researchers
where else it applies and at what resolution — and it bounds the
claim honestly: whatever coordination happens off-substrate, in the
repositories and group chats, stays off-trace (the civic-sphere
caveat of link:src-modular-politics.adoc[Modular Politics]), which
is why the trace locates and the interviews carry.

The evaluative layer then replaces criteria that presuppose an
existing institution. The 2007 criteria — sustainability, efficiency,
equity, preservation — all ask how a standing commons is doing; a
forming one cannot yet be scored on them. Formation-stage criteria
ask instead: is *boundary-setting capacity* developing — after each
shock, can actors still reconstitute a boundary, at falling cost
(link:q-boundary-sybil.adoc[boundary / Sybil])? Are *sanction
signals acquiring legitimacy* — do non-binding reports move behavior,
do filters adopt them, do exits follow them
(link:q-decoupled-vs-unitary-sanctions.adoc[decoupled vs unitary])?
Is the *shock cycle converging* — contraction depth shrinking,
recovery accelerating — or thrashing
(link:q-maturation-hypothesis.adoc[maturation])? And is the
*option space still growing* — generativity as the working definition
of robustness
(link:q-generativity-robustness.adoc[generativity])? Each
criterion pairs with an open question in the map below; together they
are the framework's answer to "evaluated against what?" for an
institution that does not yet exist.

The reusable instrument that carries all of this is a coding table
from event kinds to institutional statements: kind 1984 as a norm
whose or-else is crowdsourced, a mute list as an operational boundary
act, a relay policy document as a collective-choice output, a NIP as
constitutional text (link:concept-adico-grammar.adoc[ADICO grammar]).
The table is what lets someone apply the framework to their own
substrate instead of reading a Nostr case study — the difference
between a framework paper and a field report.

The commons school's own trajectory legitimates the whole move. Its
mature statement defines commons as a *mode of governance* —
institutionalized sharing of resources among members of a community —
rather than a type of good, insists that the relevant social dilemmas
are an empirical question rather than an assumed tragedy, and has
begun, literally, to write "beyond design principles"
(link:concept-gkc-framework.adoc[GKC framework],
link:src-governing-knowledge-commons.adoc[Governing Knowledge Commons]).
The knowledge commons is not discarded in any of this; it is
*placed*. It becomes the resource type of the demonstration case —
the setting in which formation is watched — rather than the object of
the theory, which is what keeps the paper out of the commons-advocacy
corner its critics call romantic
(link:src-exchange-theory-web3.adoc[the exchange theory's jab])
and makes its contribution an instrument rather than an endorsement.

=== A spectrum instead of a wall

In the vocabulary Hess and Ostrom built for information commons, a
relay is a *facility*: it stores artifacts and makes them available,
with membership rules, conduct rules, and real maintenance costs. The
signed events it stores are the *artifacts* — discrete, observable,
nameable representations, here with cryptographic identity — and the
discourse they carry is the *idea* layer, nonrivalrous and enriched
rather than depleted by use
(link:concept-ideas-artifacts-facilities.adoc[ideas _ artifacts _ facilities]).
Every relay operator holds what the property-rights literature calls
a proprietor's bundle: management and exclusion over the facility —
but no alienation over anyone's identity, because keys and follow
graphs leave with the user
(link:concept-property-rights-bundles.adoc[property-rights bundles]).

Boundary rules therefore do not disappear on Nostr; they multiply,
localize, and become elastic
(link:concept-boundary-rules.adoc[boundary rules]). Relays sit on
link:claim-relay-spectrum.adoc[a spectrum with three poles, not two].
At one end, the hard allowlist floor: a bounded institution,
membership set once — the set-once-boundary world the CPR literature
knows. At the other, the open pool, where exclusion must be solved
some other way. The structural novelty is the middle: curated relays
that still speak the base event kinds, so a user joins a real
community *without leaving the commons*. In Levine's terms, these are
associational commons composing on a libertarian one
(link:src-collective-action-ukac.adoc[Levine]) — and the
composition is the point, not the sorting. Fuster Morell showed that
in online creation communities, infrastructure provision is the
governance dimension that silently determines the others, and that
communities are empowered against providers exactly insofar as the
platform is forkable. Nostr disaggregates that dimension itself:
provision is plural and substitutable, so her cross-case typology of
provision models reappears as live market structure inside one system
(link:concept-infrastructure-provision.adoc[infrastructure provision]).

=== Multiplicity, and why the list is never finished

Exclusion is not solved once. The same function is solved at every
layer at once, by independent actors who never coordinate: internet
infrastructure, relay policy, named specs, client-side web-of-trust
the user chooses — open at both ends, with developers guessing
threats in advance and users adding quality after the fact
(link:claim-multiplicity-stack.adoc[multiplicity across the stack]).
The catalogue is deliberately left unfinished: new methods ship
faster than they could be enumerated, and *the unenumerability is the
finding*. This discipline has an exact Ostrom ancestry: she counted
27+ boundary rules in use across common-pool resources and concluded
that "no analyst can perform a complete analysis" of their
combinations (link:src-digging-deeper.adoc[Digging Deeper],
pp. 90–91). Ashby's law of requisite variety supplies the theory: a
governance system must command at least as much variety as the
disturbances it regulates, and the Ostroms read the very diversity of
rules-in-use across their cases as that variety at work
(link:concept-requisite-variety.adoc[requisite variety]). The
proliferation of filtering methods is therefore functional, not
fragmentation.

This order is polycentric — many formally independent centers of
decision — but strained in an instructive way: classical
polycentricity expects the centers to interact, negotiate, and check
one another, while Nostr's centers mostly never meet. The protocol
itself substitutes for negotiated coordination: redundancy without
meetings (link:concept-polycentricity.adoc[polycentricity]). And
authority inside it takes the form Schroeder and Wagner found
dominant on Wikipedia — guardianship without office, influence
legitimized by observable behavior and reputation rather than
position (link:concept-guardianship-without-office.adoc[guardianship without office])
— except that here the observable behavior is signed, so the power
gradient itself is data.

=== Sanctions come apart

Ostrom's fifth design principle — graduated sanctions — assumed a
*unitary* arrangement: the institution that monitors is
institutionally linked to the one that sanctions and enforces
(link:concept-graduated-sanctions.adoc[graduated sanctions]).
Nostr splits the bundle. A kind-1984 report is a costly, typed,
public, *non-binding* signal; a different actor — a relay filtering
on it, a client scoring it into a web of trust, a user ignoring it —
decides what it means
(link:claim-split-sanctions.adoc[split sanctions]). In the grammar
of institutions, a rule is a statement whose "or else" names the
agent responsible for sanctioning; a norm is the same statement
without one. The report is grammatically a norm that nonetheless gets
enforced — because the *or-else is crowdsourced* across many
potential enforcers with their own private incentives
(link:concept-adico-grammar.adoc[ADICO grammar]).

This reframes two standing results. The lab finding that externally
imposed sanctions crowd out self-organized ones has no purchase here:
there is no external sanctioner to do the crowding. And the
compliance requirement — that monitors and sanctioners must be seen
as legitimate "or rule evasion will overwhelm the governance system" —
becomes testable per relay, with exit as the legitimacy meter. One
anomaly is already in hand: reports from high-centrality accounts
appear to move relay filters while peripheral reports do not.
Whether decoupled sanctioning governs better or worse than unitary
enforcement — and under what conditions a non-binding signal gets
enforced at all — is the most self-contained publishable question in
the program
(link:q-decoupled-vs-unitary-sanctions.adoc[decoupled vs unitary]).

=== How it holds over time

The boundary is not a setting fixed once; it is a muscle. Open →
shock (a spam or malicious influx) → contract (retreat toward the
whitelist or web-of-trust floor) → adapt in the between (the stack
retunes, developers ship) → re-expand
(link:claim-expand-contract.adoc[expand–contract]). Neither pole
is the answer — the open pool is exposed, the hard whitelist defeats
the purpose of joining a commons — so governance *is* the retuning
between them, and the always-available floor is what licenses the
experimentation above it: a credible commitment that failure never
costs the commons itself. Each pass through the cycle leaves the
option space larger — a new spec, a hackathon output, another
web-of-trust function the space did not have before.

This is not an ecological metaphor imported onto Ostrom's framework;
it is her own adaptive-management principle instantiated. "Being
Prepared for Change" argues that fixed rules fail because they place
too much confidence in the current state of knowledge, and that
systems allowing change are "suboptimal in the short run but wiser in
the long run" (verbatim, with locator, in
link:src-analyzing-knowledge-commons.adoc[the framework chapter]).
In cybernetic terms — the terms the Ostroms themselves worked in —
the cycle is a feedback loop with the meta-level correction Ashby
showed self-organization requires, except that on Nostr the
correcting level is lateral and plural rather than singular
(link:concept-adaptive-governance.adoc[adaptive governance]). One
discipline governs this whole section: the observable oscillation is
one thing; the *maturation conjecture* — that contractions grow
shallower and recoveries faster until the loop disappears the way
HTTPS disappeared into infrastructure — is a longer-horizon
hypothesis the design is built to test, and it is always flagged as
such (link:q-maturation-hypothesis.adoc[maturation hypothesis]).

=== How we know

Method follows the phenomenon. The event trace can *locate* an
adaptation in time and sometimes catch its speed — a bonus, never the
load-bearing evidence — because much of the redundancy (relay
software, infrastructure choices, client web-of-trust internals,
interop specs) never appears in public events at all. The interviews
carry the claim
(link:claim-interviews-carry-claim.adoc[interviews carry the claim]).
What is being established is a community *disposition*: people orient
to an open problem with no global solution and keep adapting anyway.
The signal question — why are people who have seen the worst of Nostr
still here, rather than retreating permanently to a safe single-relay
community? — has its answer in the staying itself. Refusing the
global solution is the finding, not a gap. Two rules keep this
honest: "everyone adapts" never stands alone without a named instance
(a spec after a specific wave, an operator who tightened then
loosened), and the trace's limits are stated, not hidden — the
elicitation methods of the open-content literature are replicable
here *minus* their retrospective memory bias, since the mechanisms
leave typed traces
(link:src-open-content-governance.adoc[Schroeder & Wagner]).

=== The closer: the worst case hosts the best case

If governance works *here* — the most adversarial configuration, no
enforceable boundary, no global solution — then it composes with any
centralized graduated-sanction regime built on top of it
(link:claim-worst-case-hosts-best-case.adoc[the worst case hosts the best case]).
A medical knowledge commons, a town relay, a curated scientific
archive: each is a bounded institution, a centralized sanction
regime — and because each still speaks the base event kinds, it
composes onto the open substrate without leaving it, one more filter
in the stack
(link:claim-composable-bounded-institutions.adoc[composable bounded institutions]).
The medical commons is the standing instance: write-gated on its own
relay, its canonical surface curated by an institution that does
quality control and maintenance as centralized functions — while the
same signed events remain freely readable and commentable everywhere
else. No one is excluded from the conversation, only from a venue.

This is also the honest answer to the sharpest counter-claim in the
source base — Levine's observation that no successful commons ever
arose from total individual freedom
(link:src-collective-action-ukac.adoc[Levine]). Correct: and none
needs to. The governance lives in the bounded institutions; the open
substrate is what carries them, keeps exit near-free, and keeps their
claims contestable outside their gates. Operators are disciplined by
leaving rather than voting — interjurisdictional competition in which
the set of jurisdictions regenerates, because leaving can mean
*starting* a relay
(link:concept-exit-voice.adoc[exit / voice],
link:concept-interjurisdictional-competition.adoc[interjurisdictional competition]).
What the substrate credibly commits to — in Alston's terms, the
architectural entrenchment of permissionless access, pseudonymous
identity, and portability — is precisely the set of affordances that
keeps the bounded regimes honest
(link:concept-dynamic-fidelity.adoc[dynamic fidelity]). Whether
this competition yields *better* governance is deliberately left
open: a puzzle, not a checkmark
(link:q-does-competition-improve-governance.adoc[does competition improve governance?]).

=== The conversation it enters

The institutional literature on protocol governance is already a live
argument among three framings — blockchain as knowledge commons,
governance as entrepreneurial exchange, protocols as constitutional
polycentric orders — conducted, on the empirical side, over the same
two retrospective war stories, the block-size fork and the DAO hack
(link:concept-tier2-triangle.adoc[the tier-2 triangle]). The HCI
literature, meanwhile, asked for exactly this: Modular Politics
specified an open-standard governance layer — modular, expressive,
portable, interoperable — and Nostr grew one instead of designing it,
with no platform root left at the top
(link:src-modular-politics.adoc[Modular Politics]). The
contribution of this work to both conversations is the same: the
instrument they are missing — continuous, typed, real-time data on
governance *forming*, failures included — and the framework for
reading it.
