This report walks the classics catalogue published by
{{@npub18cddpua960qjy3wmw7y9gmzr4h3ajlrwq3k9jnmqzlxke4qkg6gqeyaztw}} and argues
that a curated public-domain library is not a museum but a working instrument for
thought. It is organized as five short essays and a coda. If you want the
argument in miniature, skip to {{ref:A Note on Method}}; if you want the reading
list, every section below embeds the work it discusses.

The thesis is simple: the texts collected here rhyme with one another. The
[[republic]] of Plato and the confession of Augustine are both, at bottom, about
{{wiki:the good life}} — one from the outside, as politics, one from the inside,
as autobiography. We will read them as a conversation, using {{ref:Philosophy at
the Root}} to set the terms and {{ref:Faith, Doubt, and Allegory}} to complicate
them.

## Philosophy at the Root

Every catalogue needs a first principle, and this one takes it from Plato. The
whole publication is worth transcluding here, because its table of contents is
itself an argument about how a just order is assembled from parts:

{{embed:naddr1qqwhqee4x5erqvfdw35x2ttjv4c82cnvd93j6mmx94cxcct5duq35amnwvaz7tm5dpjkvmmjv4ehgtnwdaehgu339e3k7mgzyqlp458n5hfuzgj9mdmcs4rvgwk78ktudczxck20vqtu6mx5zerfqqcyqqq82kq4qwn66}}

The Republic's boldest claim is also its most quotable, and it sets up
everything that follows in {{ref:The Novel as Moral Instrument}}:

{{quote:naddr1qqwhqee4x5erqvfdw35x2ttjv4c82cnvd93j6mmx94cxcct5duq35amnwvaz7tm5dpjkvmmjv4ehgtnwdaehgu339e3k7mgzyqlp458n5hfuzgj9mdmcs4rvgwk78ktudczxck20vqtu6mx5zerfqqcyqqq82kq4qwn66 | Until philosophers are kings, or the kings and princes of this world have the spirit and power of philosophy, cities will never have rest from their evils.}}

Hold that thought against the {{wiki:allegory of the cave}} and you have the
seed of two thousand years of argument about {{wiki:education}} — a term we lean
on again in {{ref:A Note on Method}}.

## The Novel as Moral Instrument

If Plato reasons toward virtue, the novel *stages* it. Austen's comedy of
manners is a laboratory for judgment, and the catalogue's edition is the natural
companion to {{ref:Philosophy at the Root}}:

{{embed:naddr1qqd8qee3xv6ryttswf5kgefdv9hxgttswfjk5atyd93k2qg6waehxw309a6xsetxdaex2um59ehx7um5wgcjucm0d5pzq0s66re6t57pyfzakaug23ky8t0rm97xuprvt98kq97ddn2pv35sqvzqqqr4tqd9mc30}}

The opening sentence is the most efficient piece of irony in English, and it
does philosophical work — it tells you the whole social machine before a single
character speaks:

{{quote:naddr1qqd8qee3xv6ryttswf5kgefdv9hxgttswfjk5atyd93k2qg6waehxw309a6xsetxdaex2um59ehx7um5wgcjucm0d5pzq0s66re6t57pyfzakaug23ky8t0rm97xuprvt98kq97ddn2pv35sqvzqqqr4tqd9mc30 | It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.}}

Austen's subject is [[prudence]] — practical wisdom under social constraint — and
that connects her, improbably, to the devotional works we turn to next.

## Faith, Doubt, and Allegory

Three works in the catalogue take the inner life as their terrain. Augustine
narrates a conversion; Bunyan allegorizes one; Chesterton defends the sanity of
belief. Start with the *Confessions*:

{{embed:naddr1qqdkxmmwvejhxumfdah8xtt0vckhxapdv96kwatnw35kuegprfmhxue69uhhg6r9vehhyetnwshxummnw3erztnrdaksygp7rtg08fwncy3ytkmh3p2xcsadu0vhcmsyd3v57cqhe4kdg9jxjqpsgqqqw4vqday5q5}}

Augustine's most-quoted line is the hinge of the entire book, and it answers the
political restlessness Plato diagnosed in {{ref:Philosophy at the Root}}:

{{quote:naddr1qqdkxmmwvejhxumfdah8xtt0vckhxapdv96kwatnw35kuegprfmhxue69uhhg6r9vehhyetnwshxummnw3erztnrdaksygp7rtg08fwncy3ytkmh3p2xcsadu0vhcmsyd3v57cqhe4kdg9jxjqpsgqqqw4vqday5q5 | Thou hast made us for Thyself, and our heart is restless until it repose in Thee.}}

Bunyan turns that restlessness into a road. His allegory is the ancestor of
every {{wiki:hero's journey}} since:

## The Material and the Everyday

Not all of the library is lofty. One of its most charming volumes is a history
of food — a reminder that {{wiki:material culture}} is as much a record of a
civilization as its philosophy:

{{embed:naddr1qqxhqee4xyer2wfdwehkctf3qyd8wumn8ghj7argv4nx7un9wd6zumn0wd68yvfwvdhk6q3q8cddpua960qjy3wmw7y9gmzr4h3ajlrwq3k9jnmqzlxke4qkg6gqxpqqqp64s5mywlt}}

Read beside Austen's dinners and Augustine's stolen pears, the *Pantropheon*
argues that the [[everyday]] is never trivial. This is the counter-melody to
{{ref:Philosophy at the Root}}: virtue is not only reasoned about, it is
practiced at the table.

## A Note on Method

How should a modern reader use a library like this? Not linearly. The value of a
structured, addressable catalogue is that any passage can be pointed at, pulled
in, and set beside another — which is exactly what this report has been doing.
Every {{ref:The Novel as Moral Instrument}} link above is a pointer into a
sibling essay; every embed is a whole work transcluded by reference; every quote
carries its source for attribution while keeping the excerpt inline.

For a formal bibliography, a citation record would go here — reserved syntax
shown for completeness: {{cite:gitcitadel-republic-2024}}.

Finally, to demonstrate structural inclusion, the following line slots an entire
work into this report's index as a child node rather than an inline card:

{{slot:naddr1qqyk7un5dphkgmmc0yq35amnwvaz7tm5dpjkvmmjv4ehgtnwdaehgu339e3k7mgzyqlp458n5hfuzgj9mdmcs4rvgwk78ktudczxck20vqtu6mx5zerfqqcyqqq82kqwykj04}}

The catalogue, in short, is not a shelf. It is a graph — and {{wiki:nostrdown}}
is how you draw the edges.
