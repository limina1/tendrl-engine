== What it is

[[Paul Feyerabend]]'s best-known book, and the closest named
ancestor of the essay's title. Its thesis is *epistemological
anarchism*: there is no such thing as a single scientific method;
every methodological rule, however plausible, has been violated at
some point where the violation was necessary for progress; and the
only rule that survives every historical case is "anything goes."
Feyerabend was explicit that "anything goes" is not his
recommendation but the exasperated summary a rationalist is forced
to, once the history is honestly read.

== What it argues

- *Proliferation.* Keep many incompatible theories in play, because
  facts that refute a dominant theory often become visible only from
  inside a rival one. The counterpart in the essay is
  cross-pollination as "default mode"
  (link:concept-wandering-as-requisite-variety.adoc[requisite variety]).
- *Counterinduction.* Deliberately introduce hypotheses that
  contradict well-confirmed theories or established facts.
- *The Galileo case.* Galileo won not by method but by rhetoric,
  propaganda, and "epistemological tricks"; the Copernican view was
  initially less supported by the evidence than the Aristotelian.
- *Against expert authority.* Science is one tradition among many
  and should be separated from the state as religion was — the book's
  most political move, and the one the essay's "thinking without
  permission" inherits.

== How to read it against the essay and the kasten

Feyerabend describes the idea layer of *science* — a commons with
strong boundary rules and unitary sanctions. The essay generalizes
him to knowledge under default conditions. Ostrom is needed for the
generalization because Feyerabend never asked how a knowledge
community holds quality without method; he argued only that method
was not what held it. The emergent-governance kasten's decoupled,
crowdsourced sanctioning is a candidate answer he did not have
(link:concept-epistemological-anarchism.adoc[epistemological anarchism]).
